The metaphor, made precise

In your pipeline, a syllable is a per-frame kpMS pose label — a discrete symbol, cohort-specific, sub-second. A bout is a maximal run of one syllable along the stream. A behavior (“word”) is a portable ethological name: groom, rear, locomote, …

The hard part: the same syllable can mean different behaviors depending on neighbors — grammar-with-memory. That is a discrete sequence problem, not a continuous autoregression problem.

Mission tie-in
Option A (maze-mine-syllable-grammar-candidates → curate → maze-build-grammar-rules) treats behaviors as patterns of syllable bouts. Option D (bout AR-HMM) is a separate producer — it clusters bout kinematics, not syllable phrases.

Discrete sequences & n-grams

A discrete sequence is an ordered list of symbols from a finite alphabet. Here the alphabet is syllable ids; the sequence is read at bout grain (one symbol per bout, not per frame).

An n-gram is a contiguous subsequence of length \(n\). Pattern [3, 7, 7] is a 3-gram of syllable bouts. In language modeling, n-grams estimate \(P(\text{next symbol} \mid \text{previous } n{-}1)\) — the Markov idea that only a finite window of history matters (Jurafsky & Martin, Ch. 3).

Your grammar does not need probability estimates to start: mine frequent patterns, then a human assigns behavior names. The Markov intuition still helps — behaviors are defined by local bout context, not the entire trial history.

Curation rule of thumb

  1. Name specific behaviors on long patterns first ([3,7,7] → groom).
  2. Use short patterns as broad defaults ([3] → locomote) where longer rules do not match.
  3. Conflicting names on nested patterns are intentional — apply resolves by span length, not curator gut feel.

Not the same as autoregression

Classical AR predicts a continuous value from past continuous values. Grammar matching predicts a discrete label when a symbol pattern fits. Bout AR-HMM uses AR on kinematic features inside hidden states — useful for Option D, but it does not read “syllable 3 then 7 then 7” as a phrase. For the syllables→words metaphor, stay on the discrete track first.
